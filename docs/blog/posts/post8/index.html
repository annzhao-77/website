What Does 95% Confidence Really Mean?

Python
simulation
statistics
Learning bootstrap intervals and hypothesis testing through simulated bottle fills.
Published

October 28, 2026

Motivation

I recently learned about bootstrap in class. What I find useful is that it estimates uncertainty using the sample we already have, without requiring a separate standard-error formula for every statistic. The same resampling idea can be applied to means, medians, and many other estimates. I wanted to share how this works through a visual example: how can bootstrap help us estimate a population mean, and how reliable is its 95% confidence interval? I use simulated bottle fills to build these intervals and check how often they contain the true mean.

One Sample of Bottles

Imagine checking a drink labeled 500 mL, we want to estimate its average fill and test whether that average equals the label. I simulate 80 independent bottle fills from a normal distribution with mean 500 mL and standard deviation 10 mL. These are invented measurements, not product data. Knowing the true mean lets us check whether our intervals work.

I use Python and NumPy for resampling, Matplotlib for the test figure, and browser controls for interaction. A fixed seed makes results reproducible. Our sample mean is 500.19 mL. Bootstrap estimates the uncertainty around this sample mean.

1. Build a Bootstrap Interval

We draw 80 values with replacement from our original sample. A bottle can appear more than once, while another may not appear at all. We calculate the resampled mean and repeat this process 1,000 times:

bootstrap_means = []
for i in range(1000):
    resample = rng.choice(sample, size=80, replace=True)
    bootstrap_means.append(np.mean(resample))

lower, upper = np.quantile(bootstrap_means, [0.025, 0.975])

The histogram shows these means. Following the percentile bootstrap method, we use the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles as boundaries. These boundaries form a 95% confidence interval for the population mean.

With 1,000 repetitions, the distribution is roughly bell-shaped and centered near our sample mean of 500.19 mL. Most resampled means cluster near the center, with fewer farther away. The middle 95% runs from 498.00 to 502.38 mL.

2. Does It Cover the Truth?

Now we repeat the whole experiment. Each time, we draw a fresh sample of 80 bottles from the population and construct another bootstrap interval. We repeat this process for 1,000 fresh samples and count how many intervals contain 500 mL.

Switch confidence levels using the same samples: higher confidence gives wider intervals and more coverage. The bars count covered and missed intervals. Select a sample below, or click “Show a missed interval” to inspect one example.

At 95% confidence, 953 intervals contain 500 mL and 47 miss it. The observed coverage of 95.3% is close to the intended 95%. This illustrates the long-run meaning of the confidence level: it describes the performance of the interval method across repeated samples.

3. Demonstrate Hypothesis Testing

We use the same original sample to demonstrate a two-sided hypothesis test. Let \(\mu\) be the population mean fill:

\[H_0: \mu = 500\text{ mL}\]

\[H_1: \mu \ne 500\text{ mL}\]

Firstly, we build the reference distribution by assuming \(H_0\) is true, Python draws 10,000 samples of 80 bottles from a normal distribution with mean 500 mL and standard deviation 10 mL. Each sample produces one mean. Their histogram shows how sample means vary even when the population mean stays at 500.

Secondly, we need to set the rejection region. We choose a 5% significance level. Among these simulated means, about 5% are more than 2.23 mL away from 500. We reject \(H_0\) if our observed mean falls in these outer regions: below 497.77 mL or above 502.23 mL. This rule rejects about 5% of samples even when \(H_0\) is true.

Then, we calculate the p-value. Our observed mean is 500.19 mL, about 0.19 mL from 500. Of the 10,000 simulated samples, 8,688 have means at least this far from 500 mL. This gives a simulated p-value of 0.8688, about 86.9%. A difference this large or larger is common under \(H_0\).

p-value = 0.8688 > 0.05: we fail to reject \(H_0\).

The observed mean also lies outside the orange rejection regions. Both comparisons lead to the same conclusion: at the 5% significance level, this sample does not provide enough evidence that the population mean differs from 500 mL.

Takeaway

Bootstrap gave us a 95% confidence interval from one sample. We can say that we are 95% confident that the population mean lies between 498.00 and 502.38 mL. his confidence comes from the method: across repeated samples, about 95% of its intervals should contain the true mean. Our simulation achieved 95.3% coverage.

The interval included 500 mL. The hypothesis test also failed to reject a mean of 500 mL. The confidence interval shows uncertainty around our estimate of the population mean.The test evaluates one specific mean. Both results are consistent with 500 mL.

Limitations in Practice

  • Sample quality: Bootstrap reuses the observed data. It cannot correct a biased sample or recover information missing from it.
  • Independent observations: Our resampling treats each bottle as independent. If bottles from the same production batch are related, we would need a method that preserves that relationship.
  • Known variability: Our hypothesis test assumes the population standard deviation is known. In practice, it usually needs to be estimated from the sample.
